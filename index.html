          How it works

          1. Reading your description. Words that name a style, mood, speed, instrument, key or length are read as-is. Everything else goes to a description reader trained on 5,521 music descriptions written by musicians, which picks a style, a mood and a speed.
          2. Writing the chords. A small neural network trained on the chord progressions of pop, rock, folk, classical and jazz music, including 1,098 jazz standards, writes a progression in the genre you asked for, one chord every half bar.
          3. Writing the melody. A second network trained on the melodies of 1,780 pop, rock, folk and classical songs and 365 transcribed jazz solos writes a new melody over those chords, one sixteenth note at a time. It knows the genre, the chord, the beat, major or minor, and how busy the melody should be. Phrases repeat and answer each other the way songs do, longer songs get a verse and a chorus that both come back, and the song ends on the home note.
          4. Playing it. The style decides the band and how each player plays the chords: piano arpeggios, rock power chords, jazz comping over a walking bass, folk strumming, a march oom-pah and more. The drums are built from scratch in the browser, and every spotlight follows its player's notes.
          5. Song Maker. A note grid for writing your own song with the same band, with no AI involved.

          Training data: POP909 (Wang et al., 2020, MIT license); the CoCoPops corpus (Arthur and Condit-Schultz, 2023), which includes the Rolling Stone 200 of de Clercq and Temperley and the McGill Billboard set of Burgoyne, Wild and Fujinaga; the Nottingham Music Database as cleaned by Jukedeck; Bach chorales and string quartets by Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven from the music21 corpus; jazz solos from the Weimar Jazz Database (Pfleiderer et al., 2017, ODbL 1.0); iReal Pro jazz chord changes from the parkR package (MIT license); and MusicCaps by Google (Agostinelli et al., 2023, CC BY-SA 4.0; the description reader trained on it is shared under the same license) with GloVe word vectors. Instrument sounds: the FluidR3 General MIDI soundfont by Frank Wen, rendered by gleitz/midi-js-soundfonts, licensed CC BY 3.0; every third note kept, trimmed and converted to mono.
